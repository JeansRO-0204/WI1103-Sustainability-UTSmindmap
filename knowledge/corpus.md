## [M2 s4] Open-access resources
Some renewable resources, known as open-access resources, are not owned by anyone and can be used by almost anyone.
Examples include the atmosphere and the open sea along with its fish.
Another example that is not entirely open but is often used collectively includes grasslands, forests, rivers, and underground water bodies (aquifers).

> Notes: Doughnut economics, developed by Kate Raworth, is built directly on top of the planetary boundaries framework, the outer ring of the doughnut is the planetary boundaries model epurposed as an "ecological ceiling" beyond which humanity risks destabilizing Earth systems like climate, biosphere integrity, and biogeochemical flows. Raworth's contribution was to pair this ceiling with an inner ring — a "social foundation" derived loosely from SDG-type deprivation thresholds (food, water, health, education, etc.) — so that the doughnut shape itself represents the safe and just space between ecological overshoot and social shortfall. Planetary boundaries alone is a biophysical model with no inherent theory of human wellbeing or distribution, while doughnut economics uses it as one half of a normative claim about what an economy should be for — meeting human needs without breaching the boundaries that keep the planet in a stable state.

## [M3 s4] The concept of environmental carrying capacity
Naturally, there is always a limit to population growth. Limiting factors include space, abiotic factors, and biotic factors (e.g.: disease).
Limiting factors determine the carrying capacity of an area, which is the maximum number of individuals of a species that can be sustainably supported by a particular habitat.
As the population approaches the carrying capacity of its habitat, the population growth curve takes the shape of an S (logistic).

## [M3 s5] Malthusian View – Population and Natural Resources
Natural resources have fixed limits (arithmetic/linear growth), while population growth tends to be exponential (geometric), which will ultimately lead to a crisis period that reduces the population back to levels that can be sustained by the environment.
- Thomas Robert Malthus

## [M3 s7] Overpopulation ConceptWhen Polulation Growth become Overpopulation?
Population growth
The population increases over time.
Growth varies across regions.
Population pressure
The demand for food, water, energy, land, housing, and infrastructure increases as the population grows.
Overpopulation
Occurs when the needs of the population sustainably exceed the carrying capacity of the system.
Not determined by population size alone.
Also influenced by consumption levels, technology, resource distribution, and governance.

> Notes: Notes: konsep Overpopulation dari the Population bomb (Paul Ehrlich) Sumber gambar: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3987379/Pada slide sebelumnya kita melihat bahwa populasi dunia meningkat sangat pesat. Namun, peningkatan populasi tidak boleh langsung disamakan dengan overpopulation. Population growth hanya berarti jumlah penduduk bertambah dari waktu ke waktu. Based on the commons concept, Subak fulfills the following key elements:
Shared resource: water from the same source and stream is used by many farmers.
Multiple users: a single water source can be managed jointly by farmer groups; UNESCO notes that around 50–400 farmers can manage the water supply from one source.

## [M6 s3] What is Climate Change?
Climate is the average atmospheric condition in a region over a long period of time.
Climate change is the long-term change in the average atmospheric conditions that shape the local, regional, and global climate of the Earth.
Throughout Earth's history, the climate has changed due to natural factors such as volcanic activity, variations in solar radiation, tectonic plate shifts, and orbital changes.
The climate change occurring today is happening at an extraordinary speed and cannot be explained solely by natural factors, but is primarily driven by human activities.
Changes are occurring throughout the climate system. The main components of the climate system are the atmosphere, biosphere, cryosphere, and oceans (IPCC, 2021)

## [M6 s4] Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)
Greenhouse gases (GHGs) are a group of gases that absorb heat energy emitted from the Earth's surface.
Greenhouse gases in ideal amounts are important because the natural greenhouse effect they produce keeps the Earth's average surface temperature above freezing, thus allowing life to exist.
There are four important greenhouse gases present on Earth:
- Water vapor
- Carbon dioxide
- Methane
- Nitrous oxide
- Ozone
Other GHGs caused by human activities include gases from industries such as CFCs, HFCs, PFCs, and SF6.

> Notes: GRK tetap berada di atmosfer selama waktu yang lama (mulai dari puluhan hingga ratusan tahun). Meskipun jumlahnya hanya sebagian kecil dari atmosfer (kurang dari 1% dariseluruh molekul udara), GRK menyerap energi panas dalam jumlah besar dan memancarkan kembali sebagian di antaranya ke permukaan. Ozon juga merupakan gas rumah kaca, tetapi berbeda dari gas rumah kaca lainnya dalam beberapa hal. Dampak ozon bergantung pada ketinggiannya, atau di mana gas tersebut berada secara vertikal di atmosfer. Sebagian besar ozon secara alami terdapat di lapisan atmosfer yang disebut stratosfer, yang membentang sekitar 10 hingga 50 km di atas permukaan Bumi. Ozon di stratosfer memiliki sedikit efek pemanasan bersih terhadap planet, tetapi bermanfaat bagi kehidupan di Bumi karena menyerap radiasi ultraviolet berbahaya dari matahari, sehingga mencegahnya mencapai permukaan Bumi. Di troposfer—lapisan atmosfer dekat permukaan tanah—ozon merupakan polutan udara yang berbahaya untuk dihirup, menjadi komponen utama asap kabut perkotaan (smog), serta berperan sebagai gas rumah kaca penting yang berkontribusi pada perubahan iklim (lihat indikator Climate Forcing). Berbeda dengan gas rumah kaca utama lainnya, ozon troposfer hanya bertahan selama beberapa hari hingga minggu, sehingga kadarnya sering bervariasi menurut lokasi dan musim.

> Notes: The carbon cycle in tropical peatland ecosystems usually begins with the fixation of CO2 from the air continue with its conversion into organic compounds through photosynthesis. Some of this GPP is returned quickly to the atmosphere by above and below ground autotropic respiration. The remaining net primary production (NPP) are used to grown plant tissue and is manifested as increments to the above- and below-ground forest biomass (Page et al., 2011b; Moore et al., 2011). The death part of the vegetation later on deposit into both peat surface and peat profile, When the water table is low, oxygen is present in the upper peat proﬁle, enable the decomposition of organic matter (carbon) and some of the surface peat proceeds along aerobic pathways by heterotrophic bacteria and fungi, resulting in the release of CO2 to the atmosphere. If the water table is at or above the peat surface, aerobic peat decomposition is prevented and the decomposition rate of deposited organic matter is reduced along anaerobic pathways by methanogenic heterotrophic bacteria, resulting in the release of methane (CH4). Net ecosystem exchange (NEE) is used to evaluate the CO2 exchange between tropical peat swamp forest and the atmosphere (Hirano et al., 2009, 2007). The tropical climate subjected to high annual temperatures (22 – 35 °C) and precipitation rates (1500-3000 mm) which is greatly favors decomposer organisms and also promote the plant high primary production. The activity of decomposer organism also firmly controlled by the high water level condition which restricts the oxygen supply (Clymo, 1984; Jauhiainen et al., 2016). In the location where peatlands are formed, When the plant production rate exceed decomposition Peat may form when the net organic production rate exceeds net organic decomposition rate (Clymo, 1984; Wüst et al., 2007; Turetsky et al., 2014) The accumulation of tropical peatlands has started since the late of Pleistocence (30 000- 22 000 14C yr BP) (Anshari et al., 2001; Page et al., 2004). Depends on the climatic condition, the accumulation is with varied accumulation rate of 7.4 to over 90 g C/m2y (Page et al., 2004) depends on the climatic condition. The long-term of tropical peat deposits are reported to be up to 20 m deep with average of 5.5-7 m (Anderson, 1983; Radjagukguk, 1997; Page et al., 2011), while the average carbon (C) concentration of SEA peat is 50% of dry mass (Page et al., 2011). Peatlands are located in regions with high precipitation and large flat areas (Joosten et al. 2002). In the peatlands formation areas, water supply from precipitation and lateral inflows exceeds evapotranspiration, leading to a surplus of water, permanently moist conditions and a water table near the ground surface (Schumann et al., 2007). Under the waterlogged condition, the flux of oxygen from the air to the soil is lower than the rate at which it is consumed by the microorganisms, promotes mainly anaerobically decomposition (Clymo, 1984). As a result, plant litter does not fully decay and so is progressively accumulated as peat (Rieley et al., 2005). Peat may form when the litter input rate exceeds the decomposition rate (Clymo 1984). At PSFs, dead tree roots, stems and branches are major contributors to peat formation (Andriesse 1988; Wüst et al. 2008; Hoyos-Santillan et al. 2015), while the most important factor regulating peat decomposition is a high water table level (WTL) that limits oxygen availability for aerobic decomposers (Clymo 1984; Laiho 2006; Jauhiainen et al. 2016a). Decomposition is most efficient in the surface peat above the WTL, where recent litter deposits and sufficient oxygen availability support aerobic decomposition (Clymo 1984; Yule & Gomez 2009; Sundari et al. 2012; Hirano et al. 2014; Hoyos-Santillan et al. 2016). Litter modified progressively by decomposition processes close to the surface will eventually be topped by more recent litter, and subsequently become buried deeper in the peat profile. The partly decomposed organic matter deeper in peat profile will decompose more slowly due to the dominance of anaerobic decomposition in more frequently waterlogged substrate closer to the water table (Clymo 1984; Page et al. 1999; Rydin & Jeglum 2013). Nowadays land-use change, which usually includes drainage and deforestation, is the largest driver of peat tudies of wetland CH4 dynamics from other regions have revealed generalizable patterns that provide a framework for how Amazonian peatlands might function. First, emissions differences within and between wetlands tend to be governed primarily by hydrology, which determines soil redox conditions [9]. Second, plant biomass and carbon quality directly related to the amount of available substrate for methanogenic microorganisms, and thus CH4 emissions often varies across vegetation zones [10]. Third, it has been shown that peat containing higher concentrations of plant-derived phenolics will block peat decomposition and greenhouse gas emissions [11,12]. ## [M6 s37] Too much of GHGs, how to reduce the impact? Carbon Capture Utilization & Storage(CCUS)

> Notes: Penangkapan, Pemanfaatan, dan Penyimpanan Karbon (Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage/CCUS) adalah suatu proses untuk menangkap karbon dioksida (CO2) dari sumber industri atau langsung dari atmosfer, kemudian disimpan secara permanen dalam formasi geologi atau dimanfaatkan sebagai bahan baku berbagai produk seperti bahan bakar, bahan kimia, atau material bangunan. Dengan demikian, pelepasan CO2 ke atmosfer dapat dikurangi dan dampak perubahan iklim dapat diminimalkan. Bagaimana CCUS Bekerja 1. Penangkapan (Capture): CO2 dipisahkan dari emisi yang dihasilkan fasilitas industri, seperti pembangkit listrik, atau langsung dari udara. 2. Transportasi (Transport): CO2 yang telah ditangkap dikompresi dan diangkut, biasanya melalui pipa, menuju lokasi pemrosesan atau penyimpanan. 3. Pemanfaatan atau Penyimpanan (Utilize or Store – “U” dalam CCUS): Pemanfaatan (Utilization): CO2 diubah menjadi produk bernilai. Contohnya digunakan untuk membuat bahan bakar, bahan kimia, agregat bangunan (seperti beton), atau untuk enhanced oil recovery (peningkatan perolehan minyak). Penyimpanan (Storage): Sebagai alternatif, CO2 disimpan secara permanen di bawah tanah dalam formasi geologi yang aman, seperti reservoir minyak dan gas, akuifer asin dalam, atau lapisan batubara yang tidak dapat ditambang.

> Notes: Tujuan dalam TPB tidak dapat dicapai satu satu dan sendiri sendiri serta tidak direkomendasikan, melainkan pencapaiannya bermandat: transformative (memerlukan perubahan fundamental bagaimana masyarakat dan ekonomi berorganisir diri); Universality (berlaku di berbagai negara) dan indivisibility (terintegrasi dalam kerangka Bersama). Kesemuanya berdasarkan pada pemikiran system thinking. Analisis pencapaian tujuan menggunakan cara tradisional seperti pemeringkatan (ranking) atau korelasi linier dapat ‘misleading’ atau malah membatasi interpretasi relasi antar tujuan yang sebenarnya kompleks dan saling terkait. Lebih dari itu, analisis seperti ini juga tidak mengindahkan interlinkages (hubungan timbal balik), konteks local, dampak local. Salah satu prinsip dalam TPB ‘No One Left Behind” dan ‘spillover effect’ dari praktek tidak berkelanjutan. Contoh Spillover effect misalnya pencapaian TPB 2 (kelaparan) melalui impor pangan, maka juga dapat terjadi impor pestisida berbahaya. Kalau di Indonesia impor kacang kedelai, Bersama nya ada produk GMO yg terimpor. Interlinkages menjelaskan bagaimana kemajuan dari salah satu target TPB mempengaruhi kemajuan target lainnya. Konsep utamanya adalah memaksimasikan sinergi dan meminimasikan trade off. Terdapat 4 kategori dalam interlinkages: 1) sinergi atau reinforcing: aksi mencapai satu tujuan akan mempercepat pencapaian tujuan lainnya; 2) trade off (counteracting) pencapaian satu tujuan menunda pencapaian tujuan lainnya.

## [M7 s30] Discussions:
1. Food, water, energy, and cities
Imagine that your campus or city wants to expand local food production—such as campus gardens, hydroponics, or urban farming—to strengthen food security. How could this programme affect water and energy use, business costs, access to food for lower-income groups, and the design and social life of the surrounding area?
2. Green technology or shifting the problem?
A city plans to replace fossil-fuel vehicles with electric vehicles, install solar panels, and use digital systems to manage traffic. Does this automatically make the city more sustainable? Who benefits from these policies, and who might bear the hidden environmental or social costs?
3. Waste, recycling, and consumer culture
If a campus aims for “zero waste” by adding separate waste bins and recycling programmes, is that enough to achieve responsible consumption and production? Design a fairer and more effective strategy that addresses waste from the beginning of consumption to its final disposal.
